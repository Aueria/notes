### DC component:

1. By capacitor charge balance, the net charge of the capacitor must be 0.
2. The DC component must not be passing the capacitor. Therefore, it must be flowing through the load

### Ripple

1. The ripple is divided between the impedance of the 2 branches (capacitor and load branch)
2. The capacitor size can be selected that, the impedance is very low at the switching frequency, so that all the ripple will be passing through the capacitor instead of the load.

Now we can make an approximation that all the ripple will be passing through the capacitor.

# Capacitor current and voltage

From the capacitor equation
$$
\begin{align}
i_C &= C \frac{dv_C}{dt} \\
\frac{dv_C}{dt} &= \frac{i_C}{C} 
\end{align}
$$
We can observe the relationship between voltage and current.

1. When current is positive, capacitor voltage is being charged 
2. At the zero crossing (when current goes from positive to negative), the slope of the voltage is 0

![[Pasted image 20250727202201.png]]

>[!note]
>Again, $\Delta V$ is defined as the difference between peak to average
>Therefore, the pk-pk ripple is $2 \Delta V$

# Estimating Capacitor voltage ripple ($\Delta V$)

Looking at the interval between $t = DT_s$ and $t = D'T_s$, we can see that there is a net increase of voltage of $2 \Delta v$.
By using $q = CV$, the change in charge in this case is:
$$
q = C \cdot (2 \Delta v)
$$
On the other hand the total charge can be calculated by integrating the shaded area (depicted in picture above).
In this case it is the area of the triangle:
$$
q = \frac{1}{2} (\Delta i_L) (\frac{T_s}{2})
$$
Combining the 2 above equations and solving for $\Delta V$:
$$
\begin{align}
C \cdot (2 \Delta v) &= \frac{1}{2} (\Delta i_L) (\frac{T_s}{2}) \\
\Delta v = \frac{\Delta i_L T_s}{8C}
\end{align}
$$
