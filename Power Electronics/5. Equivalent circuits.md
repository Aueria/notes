In an ideal DC-DC converter, the efficiency is 100%. Therefore, $P_{in}$ and $P_{out}$ is the same
$$
\begin{align}
P_{in} &= P_{out} \\
V_g I_g &= VI
\end{align}
$$
Using conversion ratio $M(D)$, we can express the equations as:
$$
\begin{align}
V &= M(D) V_g \\
I_g &= M(D) I
\end{align}
$$
(The current equation can be derived by substituting the $V = M(D) V_g$ to $V_g I_g = VI$)

>[!note]
>These equations are only applicable for steady state. During transients, energy storage in filter elements may cause $P_{in} \neq P_{out}$ 

# Modelling DC voltage/current of a converter

We have 3 equations at hand:
$$
\begin{align}
V_g I_g &= VI \\
V &= M(D) V_g \\
I_g &= M(D) I
\end{align}
$$
# Ideal Dependent Sources model

We can interpret the above 3 equations this way:

1. The first equation basically means that the input side equals the output side
2. The second equation describes the output voltage
3. The final equation describes the input current

Knowing this we can model using dependent voltage/current sources as shown below

This is commonly used in SPICE modelling.

# Ideal DC Transformer Model

On the other hand, we can interpret that the equations are describing a transformer:

1. The first equation shows that the input power = output power
2. The second and third equation describes the turns ratio

![[Pasted image 20250727211223.png]]

1. The dot is the polarity mark, indicating the positive side.
2. The current follows the same rule but goes the opposite direction. This means current going in the dot (primary/left side) = Current going out the dot (secondary/right side).
3. Solid line denotes ideal transformer model, capable of passing DC voltage/currents
4. In reality, a transformer cannot handle DC voltages/current and it will saturate, this is only a model

This model is time-invariant (no switching) and can be solved t find DC components of converter waveforms.
